Idea The fill-in-the-blank game

In L3 you saw how a Transformer lets words look at each other with attention. But what are all those weights actually trained to do? Here's the game BERT plays: pick a sentence with a word missing, and have the model guess what was there. You don't need to label any data — the right answer is whatever word you hid.

If you have "The cat sat on the mat", you get 6 training examples from one sentence:

Masked inputAnswer
____ cat sat on the matThe
The ____ sat on the matcat
The cat ____ on the matsat
The cat sat ____ the maton
The cat sat on ____ matthe
The cat sat on the ____mat

Free training data! This is why BERT (and PRAGMA) can be trained on the whole internet without humans labelling anything. The fancy name is masked language modelling (MLM).

Why? So what is masking actually FOR?

This is the question that trips everyone up. We're not masking because we want to fill in blanks — we're masking to manufacture a training task out of regular text. Look at the chain:

  1. We have a pile of plain text — Wikipedia, books, banking events — with no labels.
  2. We can't train without a "right answer" to grade against. Where do we get one?
  3. Trick: hide a word ourselves. Now we have a training example: input = the sentence with a blank, answer = the word we hid. The model never sees the hidden word during the guess.
  4. That gives us as many training examples as we want, from any text, automatically.

What does "hide a word" actually do? (one full example)

The mechanics get hand-wavy fast. Let's walk through ONE training example end-to-end with the actual tensors the model sees. Take a sentence and pick one position to hide:

original sentence:   "the cat sat on the mat"
positions:             0    1   2   3   4   5
                              ↑
                              we randomly pick this one

Two things happen at the same instant: we save the word at position 2 (that's our answer key), and we replace it with a special placeholder token called <mask>.

                       0     1     2          3    4     5
input the model sees:  the   cat   <mask>   on   the   mat
saved as answer (label):                  sat      ← we know this; we just hid it

The <mask> is a real word in our vocabulary (a placeholder we added on purpose — id 1 in our 8-word vocab). The model knows position 2 exists, sees the placeholder, and has to guess what was there using only the surrounding words. It never sees sat during the forward pass.

So, to answer the two things everyone asks about masking:

  • Yes — the hidden word becomes the label (the y). We save it aside and grade the model against it. Exact same role as y_true in Lesson 1's regression.
  • No — attention does not depend on the mask position in a special way. Attention always looks at every word's vector. The model produces a guess at every position; we only score it at the masked one.

How the model "guesses" only one position

Transformers produce one output per input token (every position gets a guess). But for this training example we only care about position 2. The way PyTorch is told that is with a labels array the same length as the input, where every position has either the true word id OR the magic value -100 (which means "skip this one"):

                       0      1      2       3      4      5
input ids:             [the]  [cat]  [mask] [on]   [the]  [mat]
labels:                -100   -100   sat     -100   -100   -100
                                       ↑
                                       only this position counts

Cross-entropy loss reads that array: "ignore the -100 positions; at position 2 the right answer is sat — grade me on whether I put high probability on sat there." The five -100s are why the model isn't penalised for being uncertain about the words it was already shown.

So the workflow per training example is exactly:
  1. Pick a random sentence, pick a random position.
  2. Save the word at that position → that becomes the label (y).
  3. Replace the word in the sentence with <mask> → that becomes the input (x).
  4. Run the model. It outputs a guess at every position (because that's how transformers work). We only look at the masked position.
  5. Cross-entropy between the model's guess at the masked position and the saved label → loss → nudge.
Everything else in this lesson is just code that does these 5 steps in a loop.

sentence = "the cat sat on the mat"

labels[hide_pos] = ids[hide_pos]        # only the hidden position holds the answer

ids[hide_pos] = tok2id["<mask>"]    # replace word with <mask> in the INPUT (x)

print(f"input ids: {ids}")
print(f"labels:    {labels}")
print(f"answer:    {answer}")

    Show answer & explanation

    For a sentence of length N, there are N − 1 -100s and exactly one real id (at hide_pos). Why? Because we masked exactly one word per training example. Only that one position has a target to predict; everywhere else, the model can already see the answer in the input. Real BERT masks ~15% of tokens (so multiple positions might be non--100 in a single example) but the principle is identical: cross-entropy only grades the positions you hid.

That's the data trick. But there's a deeper trick: to get good at this game, the model is FORCED to learn the structure of language.

Think about what the model has to figure out to guess sat in "The cat ___ on the mat":

  • The word "cat" should mean something animal-ish (so the model knows we're talking about a creature) → that pushes the embedding for "cat" to live near other animal embeddings.
  • The blank should pay attention to "cat" and "mat" (the words around it) → that pushes attention to focus where it matters.
  • The model needs some sense of grammar ("cat ___ on" is a verb spot) → that gets baked into the feed-forward layers.

So masking isn't really about filling in blanks. It's a scoreable task that secretly forces the model to learn everything we wanted it to learn — meaningful embeddings, useful attention patterns, grammatical structure. All as a side effect of winning the game.

That's the foundation-model idea. Train on fill-in-the-blank with billions of sentences and you end up with a model that "understands language" enough to be reused for anything: sentiment analysis, summarisation, churn prediction (L5b), fraud detection (L6). One game shapes a model; one model serves many tasks.

How How the model plays the game (step by step)

  1. Tokenise + embed (L2) — each word becomes a vector of numbers.
  2. Attention (L3) — the masked vector "looks at" surrounding words and absorbs their context. After attention, the mask vector has a flavour like "small-furry-animal".
  3. Feed-forward (L1.5) — Linear → GELU → Linear refines the per-token vector (GELU is the on/off switch that lets the network bend straight lines into curves). (In real Transformers, attention + feed-forward repeat 18 times.)
  4. Predict — score every vocab word, pick the best.
  5. Compare + nudge (L1) — loss.backward(), opt.step(), repeat.

Step 4 Cross-entropy loss, by hand

First — why do we need a new loss at all?

Back in Lesson 1 the model predicted a single number (y_pred), and we measured wrongness with MSE — the squared distance from the truth. That worked because the prediction was a number, and "how far off" was a sensible question.

Here in Lesson 4, the model isn't predicting a number — it's picking a word from a list of 8. Asking "how far off" doesn't make sense: id 2 (dog) isn't "closer" to id 3 (cat) than to id 7 (swim); the ids are just arbitrary labels. We need a different kind of loss — one that works on picking from a list of choices.

That loss is cross-entropy. It's what every classification model in the world uses, including BERT, GPT and PRAGMA. We'll do it by hand on one example.

Where do the "scores" even come from?

Before we can compute a loss, we need to know what the model is outputting. Let's peek at the architecture for a second (you'll see it in full in Step 5):

class TinyMLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        self.emb  = nn.Embedding(V, 16)            # word → 16-dim vector  (L2)
        self.enc  = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(...) # attention + FFN  (L3 + L1.5)
        self.head = nn.Linear(16, V)            # 16 numbers → V scores  ← THIS!

The very last layer — self.head — is just a Linear layer, the same kind we built in L1.5. It takes the 16-dim vector at each token's position and projects it to V = 8 numbers, one per vocab word. Each one is computed as weight · vector + bias — exactly the math from L1.5's MLP.

This "head" is the output head — a different thing from the attention heads in Lesson 3. Attention heads are the parallel question-askers inside a layer; the output head is the single final layer that turns the finished vector into word scores. Same word, different job.

Those V numbers are called logits. They're just scores — any real number, positive or negative. Big logit = the model leans toward that word. But they're not probabilities yet.

So the chain is: word → embedding → attention + FFN → 16-dim vector → Linear head → 8 logits → softmax → 8 probabilities → cross-entropy → loss

Stages A/B/C below just walk through the last three boxes.

The three stages, by hand

We'll use the same example throughout: the masked sentence is "the dog ___" and the right answer is bark (id 5 in our 8-word vocab).

Stage A — The 8 logits coming out of the head

Suppose the head's Linear layer produces these 8 numbers at the masked position:

vocab:    <pad>  <mask>  dog   cat   fish  bark  meow  swim
logits:    -1.0   -0.5   0.0   -0.3   -0.8   +2.5   +0.5   -0.5

Bigger logit = the model favours that word more. Here bark got the biggest logit (+2.5), so the model is leaning the right way. But we want a probability, not a score.

Stage B — Softmax: turn logits into probabilities

Softmax turns any list of numbers into a probability distribution (everything ≥ 0, all summing to 1). It does this in two tiny moves:

  1. Exponentiate every logit: e^logit. This makes them all positive and amplifies differences (a small advantage in raw score becomes a big advantage in probability).
  2. Divide each by the total. Now they sum to 1.
logit                 -1.0   -0.5   0.0   -0.3   -0.8   +2.5   +0.5   -0.5
exp(logit)            0.37   0.61   1.00   0.74   0.45   12.18   1.65   0.61
                                                          ^ enormous

sum of all exp(...) = 0.37 + 0.61 + 1.00 + 0.74 + 0.45 + 12.18 + 1.65 + 0.61 = 17.60

probability = exp(logit) / 17.60
prob:                  2%     3%    6%     4%    3%     69%     9%     3%   ← sums to 100% ✓

Bark got 69% probability — the biggest slice of the pie. The other 31% is spread across the seven wrong answers.

Stage C — Take −log of the right answer's probability

Cross-entropy looks at only one number: the probability the model gave the right answer (here, bark = 0.69). The loss is the negative natural log of that:

loss = -log(0.69) = 0.37

A small loss because the model was reasonably confident in the truth. The other 7 probabilities don't appear in the formula — we only grade against the right answer.

Why −log? (intuition behind the shape)

Think of −log(p) as "how surprised should we be that the model was right?"
  • If the model gave the right answer p = 1.00 (totally sure) → −log(1.0) = 0. No surprise → no loss.
  • If p = 0.50 → −log(0.5) ≈ 0.69. Mild surprise.
  • If p = 0.10 → −log(0.1) ≈ 2.30. Big surprise.
  • If p = 0.01 → −log(0.01) ≈ 4.61. Huge surprise — the model was almost sure the answer was something else and it wasn't.
  • If p → 0 → −log(p) → ∞. The model gets infinitely punished for being absolutely wrong.
That asymmetry is the whole point: training pushes probability onto the truth, hard, especially when the model was confidently wrong.

Three quick comparison points (on the same example)

If model gave bark probability…lossverdict
80%−log(0.80) ≈ 0.22😀 confident & right
69% (our worked example)−log(0.69) ≈ 0.37🙂 leaning right
30%−log(0.30) ≈ 1.20😐 unsure
2%−log(0.02) ≈ 3.91😖 sure of the wrong thing
Quick recap of why we did all that:
  • The model's head (a Linear layer) produces V real-number scores → logits.
  • Softmax turns those logits into probabilities (exp → normalise → sum to 1).
  • Cross-entropy grabs the probability the model gave the right answer and takes its −log.
Three little operations — but PyTorch's F.cross_entropy(logits, target) does all three for you in one call. It accepts raw logits directly (no need to softmax first) because the math is numerically more stable that way.
🧪 Now you do it — softmax + cross-entropy by hand
Move the sliders for each logit. Watch softmax turn them into probabilities, then watch −log(p_truth) give you the loss. The right answer is bark.
vocab  = ["<pad>","<mask>","dog","cat","fish","bark","meow","swim"]
target = "bark"   # the right answer (id 5)

logits = [-1.0, -0.5, 0.0, -0.3, -0.8, 2.5, 0.5, -0.5]

# Softmax: exp every logit, then divide each by the sum
exps  = [exp(z) for z in logits]
total = sum(exps)
probs = [e / total for e in exps]

# Cross-entropy: −log of the probability of the right answer
p_truth = probs[5]              # 5 is bark's id
loss    = -log(p_truth)

print(f"p_truth = {p_truth:.3f}")
print(f"loss    = {loss:.3f}")

Step 5 Build the tiny model

Before the code: here's one masked sentence flowing all the way through. Each box transforms the data — the shape tells you what changed.

The entire architecture is three lines of code. You've seen each piece in earlier lessons.

class TinyMLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb  = nn.Embedding(V, 16)                              # L2
        self.enc  = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(16, 2, 32,            # L3 + L1.5
                                              batch_first=True)
        self.head = nn.Linear(16, V)                                 # predict word
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.head(self.enc(self.emb(x)))
ComponentOriginWhat it does
nn.Embedding(V, 16)L2Token id → 16-number vector
nn.TransformerEncoderLayer (attention)L3Q/K/V + softmax — tokens look at each other
nn.TransformerEncoderLayer (feed-forward)L1.5Linear → GELU → Linear, per token
nn.Linear(16, V)—Vector → score per vocab word

    opt.step()                                             # 5. nudge ALL weights

Journey 🚀 The data journey — predict the next word, like ChatGPT

In Lesson 1.5 you walked the word dog through 18 layers and watched its "knowledge bundle" fill up. Now let's do the same thing for a whole sentence you choose — and instead of filling in a blank, we'll do the trick that makes ChatGPT feel alive: predict the very next word, then add it, then predict again, then again… until the model has written a sentence on its own.

Two flavours of the same machine. BERT (and our mini-BERT) plays fill-in-the-blank — every word is allowed to peek at the words on both sides of the gap. ChatGPT plays predict-the-next-word — each word is only allowed to peek at the words before it, never the ones ahead, so it can keep going forever. The pipeline is identical (embed → attention → feed-forward → head → softmax). The one real change is a rule inside attention called the causal mask: it blindfolds every word to everything on its right. (It's not just "the blank is at the end" — it's that no word is ever allowed to look ahead.) You'll see that mask up close in L4f; below we run the next-word version so you can watch it write.

Real What's different in real PRAGMA?

You actually built two tiny models in this lesson: TinyMLM (Step 5 — the fill-in-the-blank / masked model) and its next-word cousin from the data journey. They play different games but are almost the same size — and both sit a long way from real PRAGMA:

Our tiny models (TinyMLM & the next-word cousin)Real PRAGMA-Large
8–31 words in vocab~28,000
2–8 tokens per exampleUp to thousands
1 encoder layer (1 attention + 1 FFN)18 encoder layers (18 attention + 18 FFN)
~2,000–11,000 parameters total1 billion
A handful of toy patterns to memoriseStructure of real banking event streams
Seconds on a laptop2 weeks on 32× NVIDIA H100 GPUs

Same five-line training loop. Same gradient descent. Same five-stage pipeline (embed → attention → feed-forward → head → softmax). Whether it fills a blank (TinyMLM, BERT) or predicts the next word (the cousin, ChatGPT), PRAGMA is just this — scaled up, with 18 stacked blocks instead of 1.

🔬 Try it yourself

Use the masking playground above (build a training example by hand).

  1. Mask a key token instead of a value. Why is that a worse signal?
    Check your answer

    Keys are predictable boilerplate — recovering them teaches little. Masking values forces the model to learn the real patterns.

  2. Raise the mask rate toward 100%. What breaks?
    Check your answer

    With almost everything hidden there's no context to predict from — the game becomes impossible. ~15–25% is the sweet spot.

  3. After masking, what's the target, and where does the loss come from?
    Check your answer

    The target is the original hidden token; cross-entropy compares the model's probability for it against 1.0 — less confidence = more loss.

  4. Why hide tokens at all — why not show the full sentence?
    Check your answer

    With nothing hidden there's nothing to predict; the blank is the whole training signal.

  5. The same word is masked in two different sentences. Same prediction?
    Check your answer

    Not necessarily — the model predicts from context, so different neighbours can give different answers. That's the point.

  6. Cross-entropy: the model gave the true word 70% in one case, 5% in another. Which loss is bigger?
    Check your answer

    The 5% case — −log(0.05)≈3.0 vs −log(0.70)≈0.36. Less confidence in the right answer = more loss.

  7. This is BERT-style. How does GPT's training differ?
    Check your answer

    BERT fills blanks anywhere (sees both sides); GPT predicts the next word (sees only the left). Same loop, different blank.

✅ You can now say…
  • Fill-in-the-blank makes its own labels: hide a word, predict it — no humans needed.
  • TinyMLM is embed → attention → head, trained with cross-entropy. ChatGPT is this same loop, far bigger.
