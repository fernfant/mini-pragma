🎓 How models learn

model
A model is just two things: a bag of numbers and a rule for combining them with your input to make an output. Example: the rule y = w·x + b with numbers w=2, b=1 is a tiny model. "Learning" means hunting for good numbers.
weights / knobs
The adjustable numbers inside a model. We call them "knobs" because training turns them like dials on a radio. A tiny model has 2 knobs; PRAGMA has about a billion. Training is the search for knob settings that make good predictions.
parameter / parameters
A fancy word for one knob — a single adjustable number. "This model has 7 million parameters" just means 7 million little tunable numbers. More parameters = more room to learn complicated patterns (but also more data needed to train them).
training
Showing the model examples, measuring how wrong it is, and nudging every knob a tiny bit to be less wrong — then repeating thousands of times. Example: show it that x=3 should give y=7; it guesses 4; nudge w and b so next time it's closer to 7. That loop is machine learning.
loss
One number that scores how wrong the model's current guesses are — lower is better, 0 is perfect. Example: it guessed 4 but the truth was 7, so the squared error is (7−4)² = 9. The entire job of training is to push this number down.
gradient
For each knob, the gradient answers: "if I nudge this knob up a little, does the loss go up or down, and how steeply?" It's the slope of the loss. Knowing the slope tells you which way to turn each knob to make the loss smaller.
gradient descent
The actual learning step: check the slope (gradient) for every knob, then nudge each one a tiny step in the downhill direction. Repeat. Picture it: finding the bottom of a foggy valley by always taking one small step downhill from wherever you're standing.
learning rate
How big each downhill step is. Too big and you leap clear over the valley and bounce around (or blow up); too small and you crawl and training takes forever. Example: lr=0.01 is a common gentle step. It's the single most fiddled-with dial in deep learning.
optimiser
The helper that performs the nudging each step so you don't do the arithmetic by hand. You hand it the knobs and a learning rate; it runs knob = knob − rate × gradient for every knob. SGD and Adam are the two you'll meet.
SGD
Stochastic Gradient Descent — the simplest optimiser. Each step it nudges every knob straight opposite its gradient by the learning rate. "Stochastic" just means it uses a small random batch of examples each step instead of all of them.
Adam
A smarter optimiser that gives each knob its own adaptive step size — so knobs with tiny gradients still move and knobs with huge gradients don't explode. It usually trains faster and more reliably than plain SGD, which is why most modern models use it.
MSE
Mean Squared Error — the loss for predicting numbers. Take each (guess − truth), square it, average them all. Example: guesses off by 1 and 3 give (1² + 3²) / 2 = 5. Squaring punishes big misses far more than small ones.
cross-entropy
The loss for when the model is picking from a list of choices (like "which word?"). It looks only at the probability the model gave to the correct answer: the less confident it was, the bigger the loss. Example: the true word is cat.
• Model said cat was 70% likely → loss = −log(0.70) ≈ 0.36 (small, good).
• Model gave cat only 5% → loss = −log(0.05) ≈ 3.0 (big, bad).
So cross-entropy rewards being confident and right, and punishes being unsure about the true answer.
batch
A small group of examples processed together in one training step (e.g. 32 at a time) instead of one-by-one or all-at-once. Bigger batches give smoother, steadier nudges; smaller batches are noisier but quicker per step.
epoch
One full pass through all of your training data. Models usually train for many epochs — seeing the same examples again and again, getting a little better each pass.

🔤 Turning words into numbers

token
One chunk of the input after you chop it up — usually a word, sometimes part of a word. Example: "the cat sat" becomes 3 tokens: the / cat / sat. Models work on tokens, never on raw text.
tokenise
The chopping step: split the input into tokens and give each one an ID number from a fixed vocabulary. Example: with vocab {the:0, cat:1, sat:2}, "the cat" tokenises to [0, 1]. Now it's numbers the model can handle.
embedding / embeddings
A short list of numbers that stands in for a word — like secret coordinates on a map of meaning. Words used in similar ways end up with similar lists. Example: "dog" might be [0.3, −1.2, 0.4]; "cat" lands nearby; "pizza" lands far away. These numbers are learned during training, not typed in by a human.
vector
Just a list of numbers, e.g. [0.3, −1.2, 0.4]. An embedding is a vector; a house's features become a vector; almost everything gets turned into a vector so the model can do math on it.
dot product
A quick way to measure how similar two number-lists are: multiply matching slots and add up the results. Example: [1,0] · [2,3] = 1×2 + 0×3 = 2. A big result means the lists "agree" and point the same way — i.e. they're similar. This is the core move inside attention.

👀 Attention

attention
The mechanism that lets every word look at every other word and pull in the ones that matter for understanding itself. Example: in "the cat that ran was fast", the word "was" can look straight back at "cat" to know what was fast. It's how Transformers handle context.
self-attention
Attention where a sequence looks at itself — each word attends to the other words in the same sentence, rather than to some separate input. It's the kind used inside BERT, GPT and PRAGMA.
query
In attention, each word makes a "query" that asks "what kind of word am I looking for?" It gets compared against every other word's key to decide how much to attend to it. One of the three learned projections (Q/K/V).
key
Each word also offers a "key" that advertises "here's what I am, in case you're looking for me." Attention matches every query against every key (with a dot product) to score how relevant each word is.
value
Once attention decides how much to focus on each word, the "value" is what actually gets passed along and blended in. In a sentence: Query = what I want, Key = what I offer, Value = what I contribute if you pick me.
softmax
Turns a row of raw scores into percentages that add up to 100%, so they can act as weights. Example: scores [2, 1, 0] become roughly [66%, 24%, 9%]. The biggest score gets the biggest slice. Attention uses it to split each word's "focus budget".
multi-head
Instead of paying attention once, the model does it several times in parallel ("heads"), each free to focus on a different kind of relationship — one head on grammar, another on who-did-what-to-whom. Their results are combined. PRAGMA's history encoder uses 16 heads.

🧠 Neural networks

MLP
Multi-Layer Perceptron — the simplest neural network: Linear → activation → Linear. The activation (a "bend") is what lets it learn curves instead of just straight lines. You build and train one by hand in Lesson 1.5.
feed-forward / FFN
The little MLP that sits inside every Transformer block and processes each token on its own (Linear → GELU → Linear). It's literally the same thing as the MLP from L1.5, just bigger. "FFN" and "feed-forward sub-layer" all mean this.
activation
The non-linear "bend" (like ReLU or GELU) placed between linear layers. Without it, stacking layers just collapses into one big straight line — useless for curves. With it, a network can fit almost any shape.
ReLU
The most common activation: keep positive numbers, turn negatives into 0. Example: ReLU(3) = 3, ReLU(−2) = 0. That simple "kink" is enough to let networks bend and fit complex shapes.
GELU
A smoother version of ReLU used inside Transformers — instead of a hard kink at zero it bends gently. It tends to train a touch better, which is why BERT and PRAGMA use it instead of plain ReLU.

🏗 Transformer parts

Transformer
The model design behind BERT, GPT and PRAGMA: a tall stack of identical blocks, each made of attention + feed-forward. Attention mixes information across words; feed-forward processes each word on its own. Stack ~18 of these blocks and you get a powerful sequence model.
encoder
The stack of Transformer blocks that reads the input and turns each token into a rich, context-aware vector. PRAGMA's encoder is the part you pre-train; later you bolt a small task head on top of it.
pooling (mean-pooling)
The model gives you one vector per item — one per word, or one per house-attribute. Pooling squashes that whole pile into a single summary vector for the thing as a whole. Mean-pooling is the simplest kind: just average all the vectors together. Example: like turning the whole class's votes into one answer by averaging them. In L5/L6 you mean-pool the encoder's per-token vectors into one vector, then feed that to the task head.
layer / block
One unit of (attention + feed-forward). "An 18-layer model" means this whole unit is repeated 18 times, each one refining the previous one's output. Watch out: attention happens in every layer, not just the first one.
residual
A shortcut wire that adds a block's input back onto its output. It lets information (and the training signal) skip past blocks, which is what makes very deep stacks trainable instead of getting stuck.
LayerNorm
A tidy-up step that rescales each token's numbers into a sane range (roughly centered, not too big or too small) before the next block. It keeps training stable so deep stacks don't blow up.
logits
The raw, un-normalised scores the model spits out for each choice, before softmax turns them into percentages. Example: logits [2.0, 0.5, −1.0] over three words → softmax → probabilities. Higher logit = the model favours that option.

🚀 Foundation models

MLM / masked language modelling
The training game where you hide a token and make the model guess it from the surrounding context. The hidden word is its own answer key, so you need zero human labels — that's why you can train on the whole internet. Example: "the ___ barked" → predict dog.
pre-training
The first, big training phase: play fill-in-the-blank on tons of unlabelled data so the model learns general structure. Example: PRAGMA pre-trains on 24 billion banking events before anyone asks it a single specific question.
fine-tuning
Taking an already pre-trained model and training it a little more on a specific labelled task. Because it already "knows a lot", this needs far less data than starting from scratch.
freeze
Lock a part of the model so the optimiser leaves its knobs alone during training. Example: freeze the pre-trained encoder and train only a small new head on top — it's fast and needs very few labels.
foundation model
One big model, pre-trained once on huge data, then reused for many different tasks with only small additions. PRAGMA is Revolut's foundation model for banking events; GPT is one for text.
recall
Of all the real positive cases, what fraction did the model actually catch? Example: 100 real fraudsters, the model flags 80 of them → 80% recall. It's the metric that matters when the thing you're hunting is rare (fraud, churn, disease).
RNN
Recurrent Neural Network — the older approach that read sequences one step at a time, carrying a single running "memory note". It struggled to remember far-back words and couldn't be sped up easily, so Transformers replaced it. (See L3b.)
