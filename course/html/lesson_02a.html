Lesson 2a — Scaling up embeddings

A "push it harder" companion to Lesson 2. Same fill-in-the-blank recipe — we just throw more data, bigger vectors and batches at it, and watch scale genuinely help. Then we hit the wall that only attention can break.

This page is optional. You already learned the core idea in Lesson 2: words become vectors, and training pulls related words together. Everything below just re-runs that same experiment at bigger scale — great if you're curious how it improves, and it sets up why we need attention next.

Try 3 🚀 Push it harder — more data, bigger embeddings, batches.

One more turn of the dial. Same recipe, but this time we add even more sentences (with new shared contexts so animals/sounds/places have stronger category signal), bigger embeddings, and we process examples in batches of 8 per step.

What's a batch? Instead of nudging the knobs after every single example, each step looks at a small handful of examples at once. Picture the smallest possible batch — just 2 examples going through one step together:

example 1:  "the dog ___"  → wants "runs"  → gradient g₁
example 2:  "the cat ___"  → wants "meow"  → gradient g₂

one nudge  =  average(g₁, g₂)      ← knobs move ONCE, using both

The number of examples in that handful is the batch size. With batch size 1 (Try 1 and Try 2) the knobs lurched after every single example; with batch size 8 they move on the smoothed average of 8 examples at a time — steadier steps, less zig-zag. That's exactly what real BERT does to keep training stable, and you'll see the smoother loss line below. Here's how the three runs stack up:

🎯 Two lessons from Try 3, one of which is the punchline of this lesson.
  1. Scale really does fix things — same algorithm, more data, batches, longer training, and the accuracy on the same questions doubles (20% → 40%). Loss converges visibly cleaner. This is the LLM playbook.
  2. But there's a ceiling. Some demos still fail — and they fail in a specific way. "the dog says ___" gives cat instead of bark, because averaging (the + dog + says) mixes animal-flavour with says-flavour and ends up nearer to "another animal" than to "the right sound". We can't get around this with more data — the architecture is mixing context the wrong way.

That ceiling is exactly what attention removes. Attention learns to weight "says" much higher than "the" or "dog" for this kind of query, instead of averaging them flat. Lesson 3 is next.

Why animals only partially clustered (honest read)

Look at cat → plays, the, runs. Where are the other animals? Crowded out by the action verbs.

Why? In our 20 sentences, animals appear in more varied contexts than sounds or places (each animal has its own personal "says SOUND" line), and each animal is the target in only a few sentences. With this much data, "dog" gets shaped toward the words it co-occurs with (runs, plays, the) as much as toward the other animals. There simply isn't enough signal yet.

Two things fix this in real BERT:
  1. Scale. 20 sentences → fragile signal. Billions of sentences → "dog" appears in thousands of contexts that also feature "cat", "puppy", "animal", "tail", "bark"… the categorical signal becomes overwhelming.
  2. Attention. Instead of averaging the context words, attention weights them. To guess "dog" in "the ___ runs", the model can lean heavily on "runs" (a strong animal-cue) and ignore "the" (uninformative). That's the next lesson.

So everything you just learned is real: pre-training shapes embeddings by playing fill-in-the-blank. Real BERT does exactly this — same training game, same gradient descent — just on billions of sentences and with attention layers on top so the model can be smarter about which context words matter for each blank. We'll see attention in Lesson 3 and the full tiny-BERT in Lesson 4.

✅ You can now say…
  • This bag-of-words model predicts a missing word by averaging its neighbour vectors — every context word counts equally.
  • Scale genuinely helps: more data, bigger vectors and batches doubled the accuracy (20% → 40%) on the same questions. That's the LLM playbook.
  • But averaging has a ceiling: it can't tell "says" matters more than "the". Only attention — weighting neighbours instead of averaging them — breaks it. That's Lesson 3.
