Lesson 3a — How attention computes (Q, K, V)

The same score → softmax → mix, now with the real machinery: queries, keys, values, the √d scale, and multiple heads.

On L3 you saw the idea: score → softmax → mix, using the word-vectors directly. Real attention adds one layer of machinery on top — instead of comparing the raw vectors, each word first projects itself into a query, a key, and a value. Same three moves; this lesson shows where Q, K and V come from, why we divide the scores by √d, and what stacking several attention "heads" buys you.

Q/K/V The full formula

Real attention uses three learned projections of each word vector — Query, Key, Value — instead of just dotting x with itself:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q · Kᵀ / √d) · V

Don't stall on the / √d bit yet — it's just a stabiliser, and we unpack it in a moment (Why divide by √d?, just below). The heart of the formula is everything else:

  • Q (query) — "what am I looking for?"
  • K (key) — "what do I offer to others looking?"
  • V (value) — "what do I actually contribute if matched?"

The three matrices are trainable weights nudged by the same training loop from Lesson 1. Q/K/V give the model more flexibility — each word can ask different questions of others than what it offers in return.

Why one vector isn't enough (the party-mixer trick)

Picture a party where everyone is trying to find the person they came to talk to. Each guest fills out three cards. The Query card says "what am I looking for?" The Key card says "what do I offer to anyone who's looking?" The Value card says "what I'll actually hand over if you pick me." To decide who to listen to, a guest holds up their Query and compares it against everyone else's Key — a good match means "pay attention to this person" — and then collects that person's Value.

Everything earlier in this lesson used just one vector per word: we computed x · xᵀ, where each word is its own Query and its own Key at the same time. That has a hidden flaw — it's symmetric. The score for "it" looking at "cat" comes out exactly the same as "cat" looking at "it", because it's the same dot product either way. But real language isn't like that! The word "it" is desperately hunting for its noun, while "cat" isn't especially hunting for "it". Their needs are different, so their scores should be different too.

Giving each word a separate Query and Key fixes this. Now "it" can write a Query that screams "I'm a pronoun, find me a nearby noun!", and "cat" can write a Key that says "I'm a noun." Those match strongly — so "it" attends to "cat" — but the reverse score can be totally different. This is the asymmetry that the lesson's opening question ("which word does 'it' refer to?") actually needs. Plain x · xᵀ can't tell apart "who's asking" from "who's being asked"; Q and K can.

One word, three jobs. Q, K, and V are just three different views of the same word vector, each made by multiplying it by a learned matrix. Think of it as the word putting on a different hat depending on whether it's asking (Q), being asked (K), or handing over its meaning (V).

Why divide by √d?

Here's the problem the / √d solves. Longer word vectors (more features, d of them) have bigger dot products — add up more numbers and you get a bigger total. Feed those big scores into softmax and it turns too spiky: one word grabs almost 100% of the attention and everyone else gets ~0%, so the model stops blending information across words.

The fix is one division. We take the square root of the vector size — call it √d — and divide every score by it before softmax. That's the scaling factor: it shrinks the scores back into a gentle range so softmax stays soft.

See it on plain numbers. With small scores softmax shares attention nicely; with big ones it collapses onto a single word:

softmax([2, 1, 0])    ≈ [0.66, 0.24, 0.09]   ← nicely shared
softmax([20, 10, 0])  ≈ [1.00, 0.00, 0.00]   ← one word hogs everything

So in softmax(Q · Kᵀ / √d) · V, the / √d is just that shrink step keeping the left example from turning into the right one. It's a small stabiliser, not a deep idea — but every real Transformer uses it.

Go deeper: what if every word has the IDENTICAL vector?

A useful thought experiment. If every word in the sentence had the exact same embedding, what would attention do? I set all four word vectors to [0.5, 0.3] and computed the attention matrix:

             the     dog     chs     cat
the         0.25    0.25    0.25    0.25
dog         0.25    0.25    0.25    0.25
chased      0.25    0.25    0.25    0.25
cat         0.25    0.25    0.25    0.25

Perfectly uniform — every word splits 25% across the 4 tokens. Attention can't tell anyone apart, so it spreads evenly. The whole power of attention depends on words having different vectors in the first place. This is also why embeddings have to be trained first — random embeddings give almost-uniform attention; trained embeddings give sharp, meaningful attention.

Multi Multi-head attention

One set of Q/K/V lets each word ask just one kind of question. But a word often needs to track several things at once — grammar and meaning and which noun it refers to. So why not let it ask several questions in parallel? That's the whole idea of multi-head attention: run the attention formula several times side by side, each run with its own Q/K/V matrices, then concatenate the outputs (just lay them next to each other into one longer vector). Each parallel run is called a head, and each head can specialise — one might learn grammar, another subject-object pairing, another nearby words. PRAGMA-L uses 16 attention heads in its history encoder — the part of the model that reads a customer's whole stream of past banking events (the same kind of key-value sequences you'll build in L5) and blends them into one understanding of that person, exactly the way attention here blends words in a sentence.

Heads-up on the word "head." An attention head is one of these parallel question-askers — that's the real industry term, straight from the 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need. Later (in Lesson 4) you'll meet the output head: the final layer that turns vectors into word scores. Same word, completely different job — don't let them blur together.

Why Why this is such a big deal

Before attention (~2017), models read text left to right. A word at position 100 could only "remember" position 1 through a fuzzy compressed memory. Long-range dependencies were really hard.

Attention solved this: every word can look at every other word in parallel, directly. No fuzzy memory. No left-to-right bottleneck. Long-range information is as accessible as short-range.

But wait — how does it know word order? Looking at every word at once has a catch: attention is order-blind. On its own it would score "dog bites man" exactly like "man bites dog", because it just sees a bag of words. The fix is simple: before attention runs, each word gets a small extra "position" vector added to its embedding — a seat number that says "I'm word #1", "I'm word #2", and so on. This tag is called a positional encoding, and it's how the model can still tell who did what to whom.

The three steps that are the heart of every modern AI model:
  1. Score every pair with a dot product.
  2. Softmax turns scores into a 100% attention budget.
  3. Mix word vectors using those weights. Each word now carries context.
✅ You can now say…
  • Query/Key/Value let each word ask "who matters to me?" (Q), advertise "what I offer" (K), and pass along "what I carry" (V).
  • We divide the scores by √d before softmax so they don’t blow up as vectors get longer — it keeps training stable.
  • Multi-head attention runs several of these in parallel — each head a different "lens" on the sentence — then combines them.
