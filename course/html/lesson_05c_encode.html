Step 1 — Encode: words become numbers

A computer can't read. Before anything else, every word is turned into a short list of numbers — a vector. That's encoding, and it's where the whole forward pass begins.

overview Whole flow step 1 Encode step 2 Attention step 3 Neurons step 4 Output

To the model, the word once isn’t letters — it’s six numbers: [0.6, −0.2, 0.3, 0.1, −0.5, 0.4]. Turning each word into a list like that is encoding, the very first thing a forward pass does.

This is the same machine from the whole-flow page, but we've turned the lights down on every stage except the first. Watch the left-hand column: each word lights up as a little stack of coloured cells. That stack is the word, rewritten as numbers.

Why Why numbers, and why a list?

A model only ever does arithmetic — multiply, add, repeat. So step one is always to replace each word with numbers it can do math on. But why a whole list instead of one number?

Because one number can only put a word on a single line — more "cat" or less "cat". A list of numbers gives the word coordinates in many directions at once: a bit animal, a bit small, a bit furry, a bit subject-of-the-sentence. Each slot captures a different shade of meaning. Our toy uses 6 slots; real models use hundreds or thousands. These numbers aren't hand-written — they're learned during training, nudged by the same loop you saw in Lesson 1 until words that behave alike end up with similar lists.

The lookup. Encoding is just a table lookup. The model keeps one row of numbers per word it knows (the embedding table); to encode a word it copies that word's row. Here are the three rows for "once upon a", exactly as the demo uses them: 
"once"  →  [ 0.6, -0.2,  0.3,  0.1, -0.5,  0.4 ]
"upon"  →  [-0.3,  0.5, -0.6,  0.2,  0.7, -0.1 ]
"a"     →  [ 0.2,  0.8,  0.1, -0.4, -0.2,  0.5 ]
Inspect the vectors. Click a word to see its 6 learned numbers — orange = positive, blue = negative:

Seeing Read the colours

In the panel, each cell's colour is its number: orange for positive, blue for negative, and the stronger the colour the bigger the value. So a word's "shape" — its pattern of warm and cool cells — is its meaning. Switch sentences with the chips and watch each word arrive with its own pattern. Nothing is happening to them yet; they're just lined up, encoded, waiting for the next stage to mix them.

✅ You can now say…
  • A model does math, so every word is first turned into a vector — a short list of numbers.
  • One number is one dimension of meaning; a list gives a word coordinates in many directions.
  • Those numbers are learned, and similar words end up with similar lists.
