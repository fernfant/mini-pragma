UNDER THE HOODIt’s all just numbers. Right now that’s a word becoming an id, then a one-hot, then a vector — and every number is ultimately just bits: 0s and 1s, the only thing a computer can store.0100 1010 1100

Idea The big idea (forget words for a moment)

Imagine you describe yourself with 4 numbers, 0 to 10:

  • How much do you like ice cream?
  • How much do you like soccer?
  • How much do you like video games?
  • How much do you like cleaning your room?

Say your answers are [10, 7, 9, 1]. Congratulations — you are now a list of 4 numbers. Your best friend might be [9, 8, 10, 2]. Grandma might be [4, 0, 0, 9].

Dot How does a computer tell who's similar to whom?

The computer only sees the number lists. It needs a math formula that turns "are these number lists alike?" into a single answer.

The formula is dumb-simple. Multiply matching slots. Add up the products. That's a dot product.

You · Friend  =  10×9 + 7×8 + 9×10 + 1×2 = 238   (big = similar!)
You · Grandma =  10×4 + 7×0 + 9×0 + 1×9 =  49    (small = not similar)

When you BOTH score high on a thing, the product is big (strong agreement → boost). When one scores high and the other low, the product is small (disagreement → no boost). Total = "how much do you agree?"

Words Words work the exact same way

Every word in the vocabulary gets a list of numbers. The computer pretends each word is a "person" being described by secret features.

🎮 Interactive: dot product is similarity

Try changing each word's "feature scores" and watch the similarity matrix update. Words with similar scores have big positive dot products.

Twist The mind-blowing twist

In real AI, the slots aren't given human-readable names. The computer doesn't know what "is_furry" means. It just sees:

dog  →  [0.32, -1.26, 0.35, 0.31]

Four mystery numbers. The computer invents its own categories during training. Slot 1 might end up meaning "animal-ness". Slot 2 might mean something humans can't put a name on at all. It doesn't matter — what matters is that similar words end up with similar number lists, so the dot product comes out big.

These mystery-number lists are called embeddings. Each word's list is its embedding vector. Size of the list = embedding dimension: 4 in our toy, 1024 in PRAGMA-Large.

Step 1 Tokenise — word → integer

Give each word in the vocabulary an integer ID.

vocab  = ["<pad>", "<mask>", "dog", "cat", "fish", "bark", "meow", "swim"]
tok2id = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
ids = [tok2id[w] for w in ["dog", "bark"]]   # [2, 5]
What are those first two weird "words"? <mask> is the "hide this word" placeholder you'll use in the fill-in-the-blank game (Lesson 4). <pad> is "empty filler" — when you process several sentences together they have to be the same length, so short ones get padded with <pad> to fill the gap, like adding blank cards so every hand has the same number. They're real entries in the vocab so they get IDs (0 and 1) just like normal words.

Why Wait — why isn't the ID enough?

Fair question. We just turned "dog" into the number 2. Why turn that number into more numbers? Why not just feed 2 to the model?

Because the ID is just a name tag. An ID number is like a kid's locker number — it labels them, but the number itself tells you nothing about them. You can't tell from "Kid #2" whether they like pizza, play soccer, or have a dog.

Worse: the order is meaningless. We assigned dog=2, cat=3, fish=4, bark=5 just because we put them in that order in our vocab list. The model has no reason to think dog (id 2) is more like cat (id 3) than swim (id 7). The numbers 2 and 3 being neighbours on the number line is a coincidence, not a meaning.

That's the problem an embedding solves. Instead of a single nameless number, it gives each word a small card with a list of scores on it — and the model is free to put meaning on those scores during training.

🧒 Kid analogy. Picture every kid in class given a tiny card with checkboxes: 
Kid #2:   pizza ⭐⭐⭐    soccer ⭐⭐    dog-lover ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐    quiet ⭐

From just "Kid #2" you knew nothing. From the card, you can tell who's similar to whom — find kids with the same stars in the same boxes. That card is the embedding. The card's checkboxes are the embedding's numbers.

One more thing. The card doesn't come with labels saying "pizza" and "soccer". Those columns just exist; the model decides during training what each slot ends up meaning (could be "animal-ness", could be "noisy-ness", could be something humans can't name). What matters is that partners end up with similar cards.

Step 2 Embed — integer → vector

Picture this happening literally. The embedding "table" is a stack of rows — one per word. The word's id is just the row number. To embed a word, you grab its row. That's it.

emb = nn.Embedding(len(vocab), 4)            # 8 × 4 table of trainable weights
vectors = emb(torch.tensor(ids))             # 2 vectors of 4 numbers each

The embedding table is just a 2-D array of numbers. Looking up a word's vector is literally indexing the table at its ID row. No math, just memory lookup.

🔗 This vector is the "list of numbers" from L1.5b. There we said a neural net always eats a list of d numbers (a vector) — and that length is the number of columns of the first weight matrix. This embedding is that list for a word (here d=4). So whatever the input, the net ends up seeing the same shape — there are just two routes to get there:
🖼 A picture — already numbers
Each pixel is a brightness number → just flatten the grid. No lookup.
grid → flatten → [1, 0, 1, …]
🔤 A word — not numbers
Its ID is a meaningless label, not a measurement → look it up in the table to get a vector.
"dog" → id 2 → look up → [0.32, −1.26, …]

Both arrive at the same thing: a length-d vector of meaningful numbers. The lookup is the only extra step a word needs — precisely because (unlike a pixel) a word isn't already a number, and its ID is a name, not a measurement.

The hidden binary step: one-hot

Here's the part that surprises people. That "just grab row 2" is secretly a piece of math — and it's the one place a truly binary input shows up. Turn the word's id into a one-hot vector: a row of 0s with a single 1 in the word's slot. Multiply that binary vector by the table, and you get exactly that word's row. Pick a word and watch it happen:

🎚 word → id → one-hot (binary) → its vector

The one-hot is the only genuinely binary thing here: a single 1. Multiplying it by the table just selects the row. Everything downstream is real numbers.

So the chain is: word → integer id → one-hot binary vector → real-numbered embedding. The only binary input is that one-hot; the moment we multiply by the table, we're in real numbers — and stay there for the rest of the model. (Underneath, of course, even those real numbers are stored as bits — that part never changes.)

Why 4? Who picks the number of columns?
That 4 is a choice the person building the model makes. It's called the embedding dimension (or just d). Bigger isn't always better — it's a trade-off:
  • Small d (2, 4, 8…) → not many checkboxes on each card. Fast and tiny, but words can't be very expressive.
  • Big d (768, 1024…) → lots of room for each word to be its own thing. More capacity, but slower and uses more memory.
Rough rule: more words in the vocab → you need more room per word. With only 8 words, 4 numbers is fine. With 30,000 words, you need hundreds. Real models pick by trial — what works for the task.

Type a tiny sentence, see it become integer ids, then pick two words and see their dot product. The embeddings here are pre-trained on the L2 toy data so similar words score higher.

# Tokenise a sentence (words must be in the vocab above)

b = "bark"

Sentence So what's a whole sentence, then?

One word became a list of d numbers (a vector). A sentence is just several words in a row — so stack their vectors, one per word, and you get a grid of numbers: a matrix. One row per word, d columns (the embedding numbers).

Example — "the dog runs" with d=2: 
the  → [ 0.1,  0.9 ]      row 1
dog  → [ 0.8, −0.3 ]      row 2   ← 3 words tall,
runs → [−0.5,  0.4 ]      row 3      2 numbers wide  →  a 3×2 matrix

3 words → a 3×2 grid. A 10-word sentence with d=768 embeddings → a 10×768 grid. The picture is the same as the pixel image earlier — just now each row is a word instead of a pixel.

And what is "X"? X is just the name for the input you feed the model — the same X as in Y = f(X) back in Lesson 1.
  • one word → X is a vector (length d);
  • one sentence → X is a matrix (words × d);
  • the model's job is to turn that X into an answer Y (e.g. the missing word).

Training shows the model a different sentence each step — so X changes every time. One such input (one sentence, with its answer) is called a training example (or sample). The whole pile of them is the dataset; a small handful fed through together is a batch. So "training" = run example after example through the same net, nudging the weights each time. (You'll watch exactly that happen just below.)

Connect 🔗 Hold on — where did L1.5's neural net go?

Fair question, and it's the right moment to ask it. In Lesson 1.5 our model was a neural network: Linear → ReLU → Linear with hidden units, ReLU bends, and 25 weights sitting in three distinct places. Here in L2 the model looks completely different — it's just a number table with a lookup. Where did the layers go?

Each lesson teaches you ONE new LEGO. They're meant to click together over time. Here's the running picture of every model we've built so far, and what's coming: 
L1     x ──→ [ y = w·x + b ]                          ──→ y       (2 weights)
L1.5   x ──→ [ Linear → ReLU → Linear ]               ──→ y       (25 weights — an MLP)
L2     word ──→ [ embedding table (lookup) ]          ──→ vector  (V × d weights)  ← you are here
L3     words ──→ [ embed → attention ]                ──→ context
L4     words ──→ [ embed → (attention + MLP) × N ]    ──→ guess   (full tiny BERT)

L2 deliberately strips the model down to just the embedding lookup so we can focus on what embeddings are and how they get shaped by training. There are no hidden layers and no ReLU in this lesson — those come back in Lesson 4 when we put everything together.

L1.5's model vs L2's model, side by side

L1.5 — an MLP

Architecture: a pipeline that transforms numbers through layers.

x ──→ Linear ──→ ReLU ──→ Linear ──→ y

25 weights, organised as: 8 input weights, 8 biases, 8 output weights, 1 output bias.

Input is a single number. Output is a single number. The middle layers do the heavy lifting.

L2 — an embedding table

Architecture: a lookup. Words come in, vectors come out. No layers, no bends.

word ──→ id ──→ row of table ──→ vector

V × d weights, organised as one row per vocab word. With V=8 and d=4 → 32 weights.

Input is a discrete word; output is a vector. No math between them — just memory.

The constant across all of them: the 5-line training loop from L1. Whether the model is 2 weights, 25 weights, V×d weights, or PRAGMA-L's billion — predict → loss → backward → step → repeat is unchanged. Different LEGOs. Same hands.

One more useful frame: L1.5 taught you that a model has an architecture (the pipeline of math) and the architecture has parameters (the weights). L2's architecture is just a one-step pipeline (look up the row); its parameters are the table's V × d numbers. The vocabulary is the same; the shape is different.

Play 🎬 What "training the embeddings" really means

We keep saying "training nudges them" — but training what? Toward what? Embeddings don't shape themselves out of thin air. They only get shaped because we put them inside a game, and the game pushes them.

The recipe for training embeddings:
  1. Pick a game the model can play with the embeddings.
  2. Score how well the model is doing (the loss).
  3. Use gradient descent (L1c) to nudge every number in every embedding a tiny bit in the direction that scores better.
  4. Repeat 1000 times.
Whatever pattern the game rewards, the embeddings end up showing.

The game for the demo below: "I'll point at a word, you guess its partner." The secret partners are dog↔bark, cat↔meow, fish↔swim. To win, the model has to make each word's vector point toward its partner's (because that's how their dot product comes out big — and big dot product = the model's confident guess).

Over 200 rounds, the cards reshape themselves: dog's checkboxes drift to match bark's, cat's drift to match meow's. The game shapes the cards.

BERT plays a different game. Real BERT/PRAGMA train embeddings inside the fill-in-the-blank game (Lesson 4). Words that appear in similar contexts (like "dog" and "puppy") end up with similar embeddings — that's the same idea, just with a richer task and a much bigger vocabulary.

Notice what just happened. In the training phase we shaped the embeddings; in the inference phase we used them to answer questions. The model can ask "which single word is most similar to dog?" and get a sensible answer. That's already useful.

But our model can only relate words one to one. It can't yet handle a whole sentence like "the dog barked at the cat near the tree" — it has no way to ask "which of the OTHER words in this sentence matter most for understanding 'dog' right now?"

That question is exactly what attention answers. Instead of squishing every context word together by averaging them equally, attention learns to weight the context words — paying more attention to the ones that matter. Same training game, smarter model. That's where we go next.

The direction they point is already correct after ~400 steps; extra training just makes them bigger. That's why real models use tricks like LayerNorm (rescales each vector to a standard length, so no single one runs away) and weight decay (gently pulls every number toward zero each step, penalising bloat) to keep magnitudes in check.

Data 🔎 What did we actually FEED into the model?

Pause and look at what just trained those embeddings. The "training data" for the demo above is a tiny hand-built list. That's the entire training set:

train_pairs = [
    ("dog",  "bark"),   ("bark", "dog"),
    ("cat",  "meow"),   ("meow", "cat"),
    ("fish", "swim"),   ("swim", "fish"),
]

Each round of training, the model picks one row and asks: "given this input word, can I score its partner highest?" If yes, score; if no, nudge every embedding number a hair. Repeat thousands of times — the partners drift together.

This style of game is called skip-gram (or word2vec). Just pairs of words. No sentences, no surrounding context. Simple to grasp, but limited — to teach the model that "dog" is related to "puppy" we'd have to write that pair down ourselves. With a vocabulary of 30,000 words, that's a non-starter.

Is this what BERT does?

Close cousin, not the same. Both train embeddings by playing fill-in-the-blank, but the shape of the training data is different.

Toy (skip-gram / word2vec)

Training data = pairs of words:

("dog",  "bark")
("cat",  "meow")
("fish", "swim")
 ... (6 pairs total)

Each example: one input word, guess its partner. Hand-built. Tiny.

Real BERT (masked language modelling)

Training data = sentences from the internet with one word hidden:

"the ___ barked loudly"      → dog
"she ate an ___ for lunch"   → apple
"the cat sat on the ___"     → mat
 ... (billions more)

Real text. Hide 15% randomly. The hidden word IS the label — zero human labelling.

Why the bigger setup? Because real sentences give the model context — not just the partner, but the company a word keeps. Words used in similar contexts (dog / puppy / hound) end up with similar embeddings, even though we never paired them by hand. BERT discovers those clusters from raw text.

That's why our toy needed a list of partners but BERT just needs Wikipedia. Lesson 4 builds the small version of BERT — same fill-in-the-blank game, real (tiny) sentences, and now embedding + attention + feed-forward all training together.

BERT-style 🍱 OK now let's actually train BERT-style

The word2vec toy above used pairs. Let's do something closer to real BERT: feed the model 20 sentences, randomly hide one word per training step, and have it guess the hidden word from the rest of the sentence. This is the pre-training step of BERT — but without attention yet, just embeddings.

📍 Where this sits in the BERT pipeline: this is exactly pre-training — the first big phase of training a foundation model. We're using words only, no attention yet. Lesson 3 adds attention; Lesson 4 stacks them into a real tiny BERT. So this section is "BERT minus attention", training the very first piece on its own.

One round of training, walked through step by step

This is the heart of the lesson — and it's the part that needs care, not speed. Let's follow one single training round from start to finish, with concrete numbers at every step. (We're going to use tiny 2-number embeddings in this walkthrough so we can do the math on paper. The real experiment above used 8 numbers per word, but the steps are identical — just longer rows.)

Our example sentence will be "the dog runs", and we'll hide dog. Buckle up — 8 small steps.

① Tokenize the sentence — words become IDs

Same trick as Step 1. Each word in our vocab has a fixed ID number; we replace the words with their IDs.

"the dog runs"   →   [0, 7, 4]
                      ↑  ↑  ↑
              the=0  dog=7  runs=4

Three positions, three IDs. No math yet — just lookup.

② Embed — each ID becomes a small list of numbers

The embedding table has one row per vocab word. We grab the rows for IDs 0, 7, and 4. Pretend the table currently looks like this (numbers are random — they haven't been trained yet):

position 0  the    →  [0.1,  0.2]
position 1  dog    →  [1.0,  0.5]
position 2  runs   →  [0.3,  0.9]

The sentence is now a 3 × 2 block of numbers. From here on, everything is math.

③ Mask — hide one position, save the answer

We pick a random position to hide. Say position 1 (dog). The model now sees:

position 0  the    →  [0.1,  0.2]    ← visible
position 1   ?     →  [ ?  ,  ? ]    ← HIDDEN (this is what to guess)
position 2  runs   →  [0.3,  0.9]    ← visible

right answer (saved aside, not shown to the model):  dog  (id 7)

The model now has two jobs: guess which word lived at position 1, and we'll grade it by comparing its guess to the answer we saved aside.

④ Squish the visible cards into ONE summary card

The model has two visible embeddings. To make a single guess, it has to combine them into one summary card.

Here's the game we're playing: hide one word in a sentence, then guess it from the words around it. That predict-the-missing-word game has a name — CBOW, short for Continuous Bag of Words. ("Bag" because the order of the neighbours won't matter — they just get tipped into a bag and mixed together.)

And the way CBOW mixes the neighbours is the simplest thing you could do: average them slot by slot.

summary = ( [0.1, 0.2]  +  [0.3, 0.9] ) / 2

           slot 1:  (0.1 + 0.3) / 2 = 0.20
           slot 2:  (0.2 + 0.9) / 2 = 0.55

summary = [0.20, 0.55]

That one card [0.20, 0.55] is the model's "what-should-fit-here?" summary.

💡 This is the part attention will replace (in Lesson 3). Right now every context word counts equally — even the boring the. Attention will let the model weight the context words instead. For now, equal averaging.

⑤ Score every word in the vocab

For each of the 19 vocab words, the model takes the word's own embedding and computes its dot product with the summary card. Big dot product = "your card looks like the summary" = a good guess. Let's compute it for 4 candidate words:

wordits embeddingdot product with [0.20, 0.55]score
the[0.1, 0.2]0.1·0.20 + 0.2·0.55 = 0.02 + 0.110.13
runs[0.3, 0.9]0.3·0.20 + 0.9·0.55 = 0.06 + 0.500.56 ← winner
dog ✅[1.0, 0.5]1.0·0.20 + 0.5·0.55 = 0.20 + 0.2750.48
cat[0.8, 0.4]0.8·0.20 + 0.4·0.55 = 0.16 + 0.220.38

Look what happened: the model picked runs as its guess (score 0.56), but the right answer was dog (score only 0.48). It guessed wrong. That's normal — at the start, the embeddings are random and the model is bad. The next step is where it learns.

⑥ Measure how wrong it was — the loss

The right answer is dog. We want dog's score to be the highest of all 19 scores. Right now it isn't — runs beat it. So the model's confidence in the right answer is too low, and the loss reflects that.

So how do we turn "dog scored too low" into one loss number? We want a rule that's small when the right answer's score is the biggest by a lot, and big when the right answer is buried below other words. The standard formula that follows exactly that rule has a name — cross-entropy (it's the same one we work through by hand in L4 Step 4). Don't sweat the formula; the rule in bold above is the whole idea.

Right now dog (0.48) is below runs (0.56), so the loss is bigger than zero — meaningfully wrong, but not catastrophic.

⑦ Nudge every embedding — gradient descent

This is where the magic earns its name. Gradient descent asks, for every single number in every embedding: "if I bump this one number a tiny bit, does the loss go up or down?" — and then nudges each number a hair in the down-loss direction.

For this training round, the practical effect is:

  • dog's embedding gets pulled toward the summary card [0.20, 0.55]. Next time we see this same context, dog will score a bit higher.
  • runs's embedding (the false winner) gets pushed away from this summary card. Next time, it'll score a bit lower.
  • Even the and the other innocent bystanders get tiny tugs.

One round, one small nudge to thousands of numbers. The change is invisible after one round. After 3,000 rounds it adds up.

⑧ Pick a new sentence, a new mask — repeat

That's the whole training loop. Each round: pick a random sentence, pick a random position to hide, do steps ① through ⑦, then start over. 3,000 rounds in total for our toy.

By the end, the embeddings have drifted into the patterns we saw in the result table above — sounds clustering with sounds, places with places, animals (mostly) clustering with animals. Pre-training is done.

That's pre-training BERT-style, with no attention yet. Lesson 3 adds attention so the model can weight the context in step ④ instead of just averaging. Lesson 4 stacks the whole pipeline (embed + attention + feed-forward + head) and trains it all at once. But the heart of it is what you just walked through.

Steps ⏱ Wait — what exactly is a "step"? (steps, epochs, batches)

You just watched the hide-a-word game train the embeddings. Now let's nail down the three words people throw around when they talk about that training — step, epoch, and batch — one at a time. This is the part that confuses everyone the first time. We've said things like "3,000 steps" or "5,000 steps" — but what does one step actually mean? And what's the relationship between a step and the 20 (or 48) sentences sitting in our dataset?

One STEP = ①→⑦ done once. Pick one sentence. Mask one position. Compute the guess. Score the loss. Nudge every embedding. That's it. One example, one tiny nudge.

It is NOT "one full pass through all the sentences". Some courses call that an epoch, which is a different thing (see below).

What does a real training log look like?

The sentences sit in a list. Each step the loop reaches in randomly, grabs ONE sentence, picks ONE position to mask, and runs steps ①–⑦. The next step reaches in randomly again. So 20 sentences might get visited in any order, multiple times, with different masks each visit. Here's a peek at the first 10 steps of an actual training run:

step    1 │ "the bird runs"        │ mask pos 2 (runs)    │ loss 6.20
step    2 │ "splash is loud"       │ mask pos 0 (splash)  │ loss 4.85
step    3 │ "the dog plays"        │ mask pos 1 (dog)     │ loss 5.41
step    4 │ "the bird runs"        │ mask pos 1 (bird)    │ loss 3.92   ← same sentence as step 1, different mask!
step    5 │ "park is big"          │ mask pos 2 (big)     │ loss 4.11
step    6 │ "the cat says meow"    │ mask pos 3 (meow)    │ loss 3.50
step    7 │ "the dog plays"        │ mask pos 2 (plays)   │ loss 4.20   ← same sentence as step 3, different mask
step    8 │ "tank is big"          │ mask pos 0 (tank)    │ loss 2.95
step    9 │ "splash is loud"       │ mask pos 2 (loud)    │ loss 3.80   ← same sentence as step 2, different mask

step 5000 │ "the bird says tweet"  │ mask pos 3 (tweet)   │ loss 1.46

Random picking is intentional — it means the gradient sees a different example each step, which keeps training stable. (Doing the same example over and over would make the model memorise that one and ignore the rest.)

So how many times does each sentence get seen?

Average it out. Over N steps with D sentences in the dataset, each sentence is picked on average N / D times — but with a different mask each time, so the model effectively sees ~N / D × (sentence length) different (sentence, mask) pairs per sentence. Two concrete examples:

Try 1 (this lesson)Try 2 (Lesson 2a)
Dataset (D)20 sentences48 sentences
Training steps (N)3,0005,000
Each sentence seen ~3,000 / 20 = 150 times5,000 / 48 ≈ 104 times

Notice the trade: Try 2 has more data but visits each sentence fewer times. That's usually a win — the model sees more variety, which is what really helps it generalise. (Try 1 saw "the dog plays" 150 times. Big deal — it can memorise the pattern but it sees no other patterns to draw on.)

What about "epochs"?

One epoch = one full pass through all the sentences. If we have 48 sentences and we go through them all once in order, that's one epoch.

In our code we don't do strict epochs — we just sample randomly. But you can estimate: 5,000 random samples from 48 sentences is roughly 104 epochs of effective work (because each sentence ends up seen ~104 times on average).

Real BERT and PRAGMA use proper epochs and batches (process 256 sentences in parallel per step — same idea, just faster). At their scale: ~1,000,000 steps × batch of 256 = 256 million examples seen. Same recipe, more iron.

3-second summary:
  • Step — one example, one nudge.
  • Epoch — one full pass through the whole dataset.
  • Batch — handle B examples per step (not used in our toy).
  • "N steps" doesn't mean "N passes through the data" — it means N individual nudges, each on one (or one batch of) example(s).

Quick note — "cosine" vs the plain dot product. Earlier we used the raw dot product to measure similarity. The catch (from the "go deeper" box above): a longer vector gives bigger dot products just for being long, even when its direction isn't a better match. Cosine similarity fixes that — it's the same dot product, but first you shrink every vector to the same length, so only the direction counts. That's why it's the fairer "who's most alike?" measure. Why Why this matters for the Transformer

This is step 1 of every Transformer, including PRAGMA. Before attention can let words look at each other, words have to BE something. Embedding turns them from strings into vectors that math can operate on.

Three things to remember:
  1. Computers can't think about words. They can only think about numbers.
  2. Step 1 — tokenise: every word gets an ID number.
  3. Step 2 — embed: every ID becomes a list of numbers (coordinates on a learned map).
