Brief The problem

Visitor A opens a 2-bed in JVC, flips through every photo, runs the mortgage calculator, and saves it. Visitor B bounces across nine unrelated listings and leaves. A is a lead; B isn’t — and this page builds the model that tells them apart before either clicks "contact".

A property portal gets millions of visits. Most are browsing; a precious few are serious buyers who will request a viewing — a lead, the thing the business actually runs on. If you could spot those visitors while they browse, you could surface the right agent, the right listings, the right nudge.

Every visit is a clickstream: a sequence of events. And here's the catch that makes it a perfect job for everything you've built — an event isn't one thing, it's a little bundle of key-value pairs: what the user did, and the attributes of the listing they did it on. A sequence of key-value events, predicting an outcome. That's PRAGMA.

Stage 1 The clickstream data

We don't have real user data, so we generate it — 2,500 sessions, each 12 events long. Every event carries four (key, value) pairs:

action — search · view · photos · floorplan · map · save · mortgage · share · back
price  — budget · mid · premium · luxury        (of the listing in view)
beds   — studio · 1bed · 2bed · 3bed · 4plus
area   — marina · downtown · jbr · businessbay · jvc · palm

Two kinds of visitor produce very different streams. A serious buyer runs a focused search (the same price / beds / area, again and again) with deep actions (photos, floorplan, mortgage, save). A window-shopper bounces around unrelated listings with shallow actions (search, glance, back). Here's one real session of each, straight from the generator:

action=view      price=premium  beds=2bed  area=jvc
action=view      price=premium  beds=2bed  area=jvc   ...
COLD → no lead 
action=search    price=budget   beds=studio  area=jvc
action=search    price=budget   beds=4plus   area=downtown
action=search    price=luxury   beds=3bed    area=marina
action=search    price=luxury   beds=studio  area=jvc    ...

Only 298 of the 2,500 sessions (≈12%) are leads — a realistic, lopsided conversion rate. Each event flattens into tokens exactly like every other sequence in this course: [action, photos, price, premium, beds, 2bed, area, jvc, …] — 96 tokens per session.

Stage 2 Pre-train on every click

First we learn the shape of normal browsing — with no labels at all. We hide ~20% of the value tokens (never the keys) and train a Transformer encoder to fill them back in, the masked-modelling game from L4:

action=photos  price=  beds=2bed  area=jvc   →  guess: premium

To do that well the encoder has to learn the regularities of real sessions — that a focused, photo-heavy stream tends to stay in one price band, that scattered searches don't. It sees all 2,500 sessions (no labels needed), and the fill-in-the-blank loss falls from 3.55 to 1.56. We now have an encoder that understands clickstreams — before it has seen a single lead.

Stage 3 Freeze, add a lead head

We freeze the encoder (lock its weights) and bolt on a tiny head. The head does two things. First it squeezes the whole session into a single vector — a step called mean-pooling: each of the 96 tokens came out of the encoder as its own 32-number vector, and we simply average all 96 into one 32-number summary of the visit. Then a single Linear layer turns that summary into two scores — lead or no-lead.

Only the head trains — that's called a linear probe on top of a frozen foundation model (the same move from L5b and L5a). "Linear" because all it can do is draw one straight line through the encoder's output space: serious-buyer summaries on one side, window-shopper summaries on the other.

The whole point: the head has almost nothing to learn, because the encoder already did the hard part. So it needs very few labels — which is exactly the real-world constraint, since labelled outcomes are scarce and expensive.

Stage 4 The payoff

Here's the experiment that makes the case. We hand each model a shrinking number of labelled sessions and compare the frozen-pretrained probe against an identical encoder trained from scratch (no pre-training). The metric that matters is recall on leads: of the real buyers, how many did we catch? (Our 500-session test set holds 57 real leads — if the model flags 52 of them, that's 52 / 57 ≈ 91% recall.)

Play it. Drag the number of labelled sessions and watch recall on real buyers move — the pre-trained edge is biggest when labels are scarce:

Pre-trained 91% From scratch 28%

Why Why it works

The encoder spent Stage 2 learning "what does browsing look like?" from thousands of unlabelled sessions. By the time it meets its first label, the work of representing a session as a meaningful vector is already done — the probe only has to draw a line between two clouds of points. From-scratch, the model has to learn both at once from a handful of examples, and starves.

And note we report recall, not accuracy. With only 12% leads, a lazy model that screams "no-lead" for everyone scores 88% accuracy and is useless — it catches zero buyers. Recall asks the real question: of the people who would have asked to see a home, how many did we find?

Your turn Make it yours

  • Add an event type. Give serious buyers a virtual_tour or repeat_visit action and watch what the probe learns.
  • Make it harder. Add label noise (some hot sessions don't convert; some cold ones do) and see how far recall holds up — real life is never this clean.
  • Change the question. Predict the buyer's price band instead of lead/no-lead, or how soon they'll convert. Same encoder, new head.
  • Stress the labels. Push the labelled set down to 10, or 5. Where does the pre-trained advantage finally break?

Trace Where the lead decision actually comes from

The frozen encoder has already boiled each visitor's whole clickstream down to a few feature numbers. The probe — the only part we trained, on those ~20 labels — just multiplies each feature by a weight, adds them up, and squashes the total into a probability.
